# Norman Muhwezi Portfolio: V2 Implementation Brief for Claude Code

## 0. Purpose

This document is the source of truth for the next version of Norman Muhwezi's portfolio website.

The goal is **not** to redesign the site from scratch. The existing visual language is strong and should be preserved: editorial, restrained, senior, distinctive, and clearly not a generic AI portfolio.

The goal of V2 is to improve **recruiter conversion, positioning, evidence, information architecture, and storytelling** so that the site communicates one clear professional proposition:

> **Norman is a senior digital transformation leader who has spent 15+ years turning ambitious technology ideas into systems that work at scale across Africa and emerging markets, and who now advises on AI adoption.**

The site should make a recruiter understand within 10-20 seconds:
1. what Norman does;
2. the level at which he operates;
3. what makes his career distinctive;
4. the scale of what he has delivered;
5. why his AI adoption advisory work is credible;
6. what kind of roles/advisory work he is suited for.

# 25. Language and anti-AI-slop rules

These rules apply to **all public-facing website copy**, including headings, labels, buttons, metadata, case studies, accordion text and alt text.

The writing should sound like Norman speaking in a strong interview: specific, calm, experienced and easy to understand.

## Natural voice

Public-facing copy should read like Norman explaining his work to an intelligent recruiter in conversation.

- Use everyday professional English.
- Prefer one clear idea per sentence.
- Use contractions where they sound natural, such as `I've`, `I'm` and `it's`.
- Use `I` when Norman's personal role matters.
- Use `we` only when the work was genuinely collective and the sentence benefits from saying so.
- Name the real thing instead of replacing it with an abstract label. Say `telecom operators`, `government teams`, `classroom construction`, `U-Report`, `vendors` or `users` when those are the actual nouns.
- Explain specialist terms in plain English the first time they appear.
- Vary sentence length naturally.
- Allow a little personality. The copy should feel composed, not polished to the point of sounding manufactured.
- Keep short factual details that make the work feel lived-in and specific.
- Prefer `I helped structure the partnership` over `partnership architecture was established`.
- Prefer `working with telecom operators` over `ecosystem stakeholder engagement`.
- Prefer `tracking how people used the platform` over `monitoring adoption metrics` when both mean the same thing.
- Prefer `what needs to improve` over `the target operating outcome` when precision is not lost.
- Read every paragraph aloud. If it sounds unnatural when spoken, rewrite it.
- Keep the tone professional. Natural does not mean casual, chatty or playful.
- Do not add rhetorical questions, filler phrases or conversational asides simply to make the copy sound human.

## Patterns that often sound machine-written

Avoid:
- dense strings of abstract nouns;
- every section having a slogan plus three supporting nouns;
- overly symmetrical sentence structures;
- repeated `strategy, execution and scale` type triplets;
- repeated `X, Y and Z` constructions;
- sentences that sound like consultancy slide headings;
- excessive use of `operating model`, `governance`, `ecosystem`, `value case`, `transformation` and `scale`;
- unnecessary capitalization for emphasis;
- polished claims with no human detail;
- repeating the same professional label in adjacent sections.

Use technical language when it is the clearest language, then return to normal English.

## Positive and confident framing

All public-facing copy should use affirmative construction.

- Lead with capability, action, decision, progress and outcome.
- Describe the operating context without dramatizing difficulty.
- Use `context`, `objective`, `design`, `delivery`, `evidence` and `scale` where they fit naturally.
- State Norman's contribution directly: `I led`, `I built`, `I designed`, `I managed`, `I advised`, `I scaled`.
- Use present-tense confidence for current positioning: `I lead`, `I advise`, `I apply`.
- Keep the tone composed. Confidence should come from evidence rather than superlatives.
- Frame future direction as an active extension of existing work.
- Keep challenge language inside factual project context only when the term is genuinely useful.
- Public-facing headings should feel constructive and forward-moving.

Avoid defensive or deficit framing in public copy, including phrases such as:
- `I am not...`
- `I don't...`
- `I am trying to...`
- `I hope to...`
- `I am aspiring to...`
- `I am moving into...`
- `despite...`
- `although I...`
- `the problem is...`
- `the hard part is...`
- `difficult...`
- `limitations...`
- `weakness...`
- `lack of...`

When a case study needs tension, express it through the **context and objective**, then move quickly into Norman's decisions and delivery.

- Never use the punctuation mark known as an em dash anywhere in public-facing copy.
- Avoid rhetorical contrast formulas such as:
  - "not X, but Y";
  - "more than X, it is Y";
  - "X is not just about Y";
  - "rather than simply...";
  - "it is less about X and more about Y".
- Avoid hedging words in claims:
  - may;
  - might;
  - could;
  - perhaps;
  - potentially;
  - arguably;
  - likely.
- If a claim is uncertain, leave it out or flag it for factual confirmation. Do not soften an uncertain claim with hedging.
- Avoid repeated three-part slogan structures.
- Avoid repeated "from X to Y" constructions. One strong use is enough.
- Avoid beginning consecutive sentences with the same pattern.
- Avoid unnecessary colons in headings and body copy.
- Prefer short declarative sentences.
- Prefer plain verbs such as `led`, `built`, `designed`, `managed`, `scaled`, `secured`, `integrated`, `tested`, `advised`, `delivered`.
- Prefer exact numbers and concrete nouns over adjectives.
- Use first person selectively. Case studies can use concise factual narrative.
- Keep sentences at a natural spoken length. Break long sentences instead of stacking clauses.

## Words and phrases to avoid

Do not use these unless the term is part of an official proper name or a technically necessary phrase:

- leverage / leveraging
- unlock / unlocking
- cutting-edge
- game-changing
- future-ready
- seamless / seamlessly
- transformative solutions
- AI-powered transformation
- harness the power of
- empower / empowering
- revolutionise / revolutionize
- reimagine
- next-generation
- world-class
- dynamic
- robust
- holistic
- impactful
- pivotal
- journey
- landscape
- realm
- delve
- foster
- spearhead
- visionary
- thought leader
- synergise / synergy
- accelerate value
- drive value
- value creation as a vague claim
- at the intersection of
- innovation ecosystem as a vague label
- digital-first
- best-in-class
- end-to-end when a more precise description exists

`Digital transformation`, `AI adoption`, `operating model`, `platform`, `programme`, `infrastructure`, and `innovation` are legitimate domain terms. Use them only where they describe the work accurately. Do not repeat them as filler.

## Copy test

Before accepting any new public-facing sentence, run these checks:

1. **Specificity:** Does it name a real problem, action, decision, system or outcome?
2. **Ownership:** Is Norman's role clear without overstating it?
3. **Evidence:** Is there a fact, mechanism or concrete context behind the claim?
4. **Voice:** Would Norman comfortably say this sentence in an interview?
5. **Uniqueness:** Could the same sentence appear unchanged on thousands of LinkedIn profiles?

11. **Read-aloud check:** Does it sound natural when spoken?
12. **Abstraction check:** Can an abstract phrase be replaced by the actual person, system, action or result?

If the voice, uniqueness, punctuation, formula, hedge, buzzword, read-aloud or abstraction checks fail, rewrite the sentence.
